Pum

pum.me

A period and symptom tracker built for endometriosis, for iPhone. Free, no account, and the data stays on her phone.

Pum is a SwiftUI app over a Rust engine compiled to a static library, with UniFFI-generated Swift bindings. The engine holds cycle detection, the insight models and the hypothesis ledger, so the same code runs in the app, in a macOS test harness and in a simulation CLI.

Its insights are n-of-1 models over her own history: whether poor sleep precedes pain days for her, say, using a case-crossover estimator, so each person is compared with herself rather than with a population average. An eval harness gates them. A candidate model must show more statistical power than its baseline on simulated data, at a false-positive rate no higher than 5%, before it replaces it.

Pum calls no LLM. Instead she can export everything, or chosen sections, ready to paste into an assistant, and the export says what it includes, what she left out, and what she hasn’t logged, so the assistant doesn’t mistake missing data for absent symptoms.

I run its production alone: Rust services with Postgres behind nginx and systemd, serving the marketing site, an admin dashboard, a crash collector and the end-to-end encrypted sync server for Pum Circle.

Project MAGI

projectmagi.app

Why the UFO files? Because it is a fascinating and rich problem — over 100,000 documents, some natively digital PDFs (easy to read), some damaged and faded scans nearly 80 years old. Different categories of documents: tables, material research, teletyped military reports, handwritten scribbles, administrative leaflets, newspaper clippings.

Within these documents are eyewitness accounts, testimony from high-ranking officials and members of the private sector, minutes from meetings with famous physicists, and the ramblings of crackpots and excited reporters.

Furthermore — some of the documents reference the same events, the same people and the same places, but they have been scattered across the files of different departments and organisations, in different formats, and kept classified for decades.

The goal of Project MAGI is to digest these documents, construct a mesh that draws what links can be drawn, perform summary and entity extraction, and make this data available to humans in a powerful GUI and to AI agents in an efficient MCP.

Every claim back to its page

  1. The record arrives as bulk archives and page viewers. MAGI fetches every release and files each document under its own hash.
  2. OCR reads each page and scores it clean or garbled or empty. Bad pages are counted so nothing is hidden.
  3. Entities are pulled from the text. People and places and agencies and dates.
  4. They join one mesh. Every claim in it resolves to a page and a span in the original.
  5. Source text and editors’ notes and readers’ theories never share a table. Coverage is checked against each release’s own index.

The aim is a record anyone can check. It takes no position on what UAPs are.

Seafaring map

Can free wind and ocean data predict where Bronze Age harbours still lie buried around the Arabian Sea?

I built a seasonal drift and sailing model from ERA5 wind reanalysis and GLORYS ocean currents, divided the ancient coast into 499 segments, and scored each by its position in the network that results.

The test was whether network position predicts where radiocarbon-dated Bronze Age activity actually is. The dates were kept blind. The pre-registration went through four rounds of adversarial review by a separate referee agent, a power study on synthetic worlds and a two-look design, and was sealed to a git commit hash.

Some of the dates exist only in print, so I went to the stacks at the University of Queensland and the State Library, with Claude on my phone directing which tables to photograph; each page was read by OCR and verified against its image before entering the corpus.

Look 1 ran at 42 events: p = 0.186 against a threshold of 0.02. It is recorded as uninformative, not as evidence either way, and the remaining alpha carries to Look 2.

Treasure hunting with free data

  1. Start with the coast as it stood four thousand years ago. Cut it into 499 stretches.
  2. Add the weather. Decades of free wind and ocean data show where a boat would drift or sail in every month.
  3. Score each stretch by how much of that traffic passes it. A high score is a natural harbour on a busy sea road.
  4. Lay down the Bronze Age sites already found. Their dates stayed hidden while the model was built.
  5. High scores with no site yet are the prediction. They are where to dig next.
  6. The test was sealed before it ran. The first look at 42 dated events was inconclusive. The second waits for 55.
